An Education of the Soul

How the playlist I win teaches, one song at a time, and why its order matters.

I win is a playlist of 39 songs in eight sections. Read in order, it works like a course. It begins at war with “them” and ends speaking to “you”. Along the way it traces the path Carl Jung described for the soul: the mask, the shadow, the soul-guide and the Self. This course shows that path in the data, places it in the research on playlists and in Spotify’s own profiles of listeners, and lets you test whether a machine could have drawn it.

Module 1 · The idea

What does it mean to read a playlist as a course?

Short answer

A playlist puts songs in an order. When the order is chosen with care, each song becomes a lesson and the whole playlist becomes a course.

Analysis

Music was the soul’s first teacher long before it was a stream. In Plato’s Republic the education of the young begins with music, because “rhythm and harmony find their way into the inward places of the soul, on which they mightily fasten”. Keats, in the letter above, turned the whole world into a school: the heart is the book the soul learns to read from, and the soul is what the reading makes. I win can be read in the same spirit. Its thirty-nine songs are the lessons, their order is the curriculum, and the curator is the teacher who decides what comes next, when to raise the voice, when to fall silent and when to come back to a lesson not yet learned.

The sociologist Tia DeNora, listening to people listening, found them working “like disc jockeys to themselves”: they used music to move from one state to another and to tell themselves who they are. Curation takes that one step further. A song alone is a feeling. A song placed after another becomes a sentence. Thirty-nine songs in a chosen order become an education, a word often traced to the Latin for leading out.

I win has 39 songs. Its curator divided them into eight sections and named each one. This course treats each section as a lesson and asks what it teaches.

Three ways to read each song, and one test

Reading 1 · address

Whom it speaks to

A close reading gives each song an addressee: “them” (rivals, enemies), “you” (someone close), “us”, or the self. Four tracks have no words to address anyone.

Reading 2 · themes

What its words talk about

A lexicon of 617 weighted words sorts the lyrics into 14 themes, such as loyalty, shelter, or water and night. The 32 songs with at least 20 words are scored.

Reading 3 · figures

Which of Jung’s figures leads

The themes are grouped into the four figures Jung placed on the soul’s path. A level of 1 is the playlist’s average; 2 is twice the average.

The test · shuffle

What the order adds

The same songs are dealt into random orders thousands of times. A pattern that appears in the real order and rarely in random ones was made by the curator.

Jung’s four figures, in plain words

The themes listed under each figure follow Jung’s descriptions. Module 5 shows that the data group a few themes differently.

What to remember
  • The songs are the lessons, the order is the curriculum, and the curator is the teacher.
  • Each song is read three ways: whom it speaks to, what it talks about, and which figure leads.
  • The shuffle test separates what the songs bring from what the order adds.
Module 2 · The research

What do we already know about playlists?

Short answer

Researchers have studied why people make playlists, how they order them, how machines order them, and how platforms label listeners. Few have read the order of one personal playlist as a story of the self and tested that reading against chance.

2.1 · Meaning

Music as a technology of the self

The sociologist Tia DeNora interviewed fifty-two women in Britain and the United States about music in their daily lives (Music in Everyday Life, 2000). She found them working “like disc jockeys to themselves”. They chose music to calm down, to focus, to get ready for an evening, or to remember someone. In her words, music became “a technology for spinning the apparently continuous tale of who one is”.

DeNora adds a precise idea. Much of our identity work is done for others: we present ourselves to colleagues, friends and family. Listening alone is identity work done for oneself, a form of introjection, “a presentation of self to self”. A private playlist is a clear case of it. Her book also recalls Aristotle’s view that music has “the power of producing an effect on the character of the soul”. That is the starting point of this course.

2.2 · Craft

The mix as an art

In 2006, Sally Jo Cunningham, David Bainbridge and Annette Falconer studied how people build playlists. They combined interviews with 115 requests for help posted on the Art of the Mix forum. They found two kinds of lists. Personal-use playlists are loose collections for a mood or an activity. Formal mixes have an organising principle and a deliberate order. About a quarter of the mixes centred on an artist or a genre, a quarter on an event, a fifth on romance, and about one in six set out to tell a message or a story.

Mix makers share loose rules. Open with a good song, but not the best one. Choose the last song with care, because it is the one people remember. Avoid more than two songs in a row by the same artist. Then they break the rules on purpose: “sometimes an abrupt change-up can be like an alarm clock going off in a mix”. One interviewee put it simply: making a playlist is “more of an art than a science”.

The critic Rob Sheffield, in Love Is a Mix Tape (2007), adds that a mix changes the songs themselves. He would rather hear a Frank Sinatra song between Run-DMC and Bananarama than between two other Sinatra songs. A song takes a new meaning from its neighbours. I win is a formal mix in this sense: it has an organising principle, a chosen order, and turns placed on purpose.

2.3 · Meaning

Curation as context creation

In his doctoral thesis on personal streaming playlists (2019), Onur Sesigür interviewed curators and music-industry professionals in Turkey. He defines playlist curation as context creation: a song placed in a playlist carries the context of the person who placed it there. His participants describe their playlists as diaries, indexes and even a museum of their own lives. One calls them “islands”: in a cloud where nothing truly belongs to you, the playlist is a space that does. Another keeps “cornerstone” playlists to “return to [his] original self”.

Sesigür concludes that the link between identity and music “cannot be deduced to a mere algorithm of preferences”, and that curation “has the potential to create non-dominant discourse”. These are the two claims this course puts to the test with numbers.

One of Sesigür’s participants, a DJ, spent months “educating” Spotify’s algorithm. He avoided making new playlists so as not to “alter the character Spotify knows me as”.In that story, the listener educates the machine. In I win, the playlist educates the listener.

2.4 · Machines

How machines order songs

Automatic playlist generation, surveyed by Geoffray Bonnin and Dietmar Jannach (2014), mostly picks each next song for its similarity to the previous one or to a seed song. Smooth transitions count as a mark of quality. Harald Schweiger, Emilia Parada-Cabaleiro and Markus Schedl (2025) gave that smoothness a formal measure, coherence, and applied it to more than 650,000 user playlists. Longer playlists turned out more coherent. Playlists full of popular hits, and playlists edited many times, were less so. The authors propose an algorithm that reorders a playlist toward typical coherence, and they call for future work on curators’ personality and listening context.

Media scholars read the same machines critically. Robert Prey (2018) calls their output algorithmic individuation: the platform assembles a statistical individual from what you played, and then addresses you as that individual. Nick Seaver (2019) shows that recommender designers often think of their systems as traps, built to capture attention and keep it. Working with Spotify’s own data, Ashton Anderson and colleagues (2020) found that listening driven by recommendations is less varied than listening people organise themselves, and that users who become more varied over time rely less on the algorithm.

2.5 · Profiles

How Spotify describes its listeners

Spotify itself describes listeners as characters. In its 2023 year-end recap, “Me in 2023”, each user received one of twelve listening characters. In September 2023 it launched daylist, a playlist that renames itself several times a day with a mood, a micro-genre and a time of day. Wrapped 2025 added a Listening Age, based on the release years of the songs you play most, and Clubs such as Club Serotonin or Soft Hearts Club, with roles like Curator, Archivist or Scout.

Show characters that read

The twelve “Me in 2023” listening characters, paraphrased from Spotify’s descriptions. Select one to compare what it reads with what it would miss in I win.

What the profiles read, and what they miss. All twelve characters read habits: how often you replay, skip, explore, finish albums or make playlists. None reads what the songs say, in which order they come, or to whom they speak. The Time Traveller shows the gap well. Return and repetition is the largest theme in the lyrics of I win, yet Spotify would only see whether you replay a song. It would never see songs about coming back.

Psychology reads taste more carefully, but still taste. Peter Rentfrow and Samuel Gosling (2003) found four broad dimensions of musical preference: reflective and complex, intense and rebellious, upbeat and conventional, and energetic and rhythmic. Each relates to personality traits; for example, a liking for reflective and complex music goes with openness to experience. These studies describe what people like, not how they arrange it.

Labels flatter. In 1949 the psychologist Bertram Forer gave his students one vague personality sketch, copied for everyone. They rated it as a very accurate description of themselves. Pop-psychology labels, from horoscopes to brand archetypes (Mark and Pearson, 2001) and “shadow work” journals, work in the same way. A label that cannot fail teaches little. That is why every label in this course comes with a count that can be checked and a shuffle that can fail.

2.6 · Therapy

Sequenced listening as an inner journey

Music therapy has used deliberately ordered listening for decades. In the Bonny Method of Guided Imagery and Music, developed by Helen Bonny in the 1970s, a therapist plays a programme of classical pieces, sequenced in advance, while the client describes the images that arise. The programme has a shape: it opens, builds, peaks and returns. The Jungian music therapist Margareta Wärja (1994) described this kind of work as a spiralling path of individuation.

I win can be read as a programme of this kind, written by the listener herself with songs from a streaming catalogue. The course reads it with the tools listed below. Each one is explained at the point where it is used.

2.7 · Overview

Who has looked at playlists, and what they looked at

    The gap. Put together, these studies explain why people make playlists, how they order them, how machines order them, and how platforms label listeners. What is missing is a reading of the order of one personal playlist as a psychological story, with a test of that reading against chance and against machines. This course tries to fill that gap. Module 9 lists what it adds.

    What to remember
    • Listeners use music as a technology of the self (DeNora); mix makers treat order as an art (Cunningham et al.).
    • Curation creates context, and identity cannot be reduced to an algorithm of preferences (Sesigür).
    • Machines favour smooth, similar sequences (Bonnin and Jannach; Schweiger et al.), and platforms describe listeners by their habits (Spotify’s characters).
    • No profile reads the order of a playlist as a story. That is what this course does.
    Module 3 · The order

    Whom does each song speak to?

    Short answer

    At the start, almost every song speaks to enemies. By the end, every song speaks to someone close.

    Select any song to read its card. Address comes from a close reading of the lyrics; hatched tiles are acts read from their title and sound.

    Analysis

    The sequence above follows whom each song addresses, and it shows the main thing this school teaches: not a new vocabulary but a new addressee. The first term is spoken to enemies. All of the opening nine songs, and eleven of the first seventeen, speak to “them”. The two signals stand like the doors of the course, a devilish sign inside the war cry (6) and the spoken warning that closes the pact (12). After the warning the colour of the sequence changes, and songs to “you” and “us” take over. Only two of the last twenty-two songs still speak to “them”: the charm of Lemon (23) and the last projection of Not Like Us (27). The songs that speak to the self sit at the hinges, in the pact, in the counter-magic and once in the return to dialogue, where the soul turns back on itself before speaking outward again. The sanctuary is the only section with neither an enemy nor a silence: every song speaks to someone close.

    Concept
    Projection
    C. G. Jung, Aion (1951)

    What it says

    Projection is the habit of seeing in other people what we cannot accept in ourselves. Jung saw it as the usual way the shadow first appears: as an enemy who seems to carry all the darkness, while we keep the light. Projections fade only when they are recognised and taken back.

    Where it shows in I win

    The first lesson speaks only to “them”: rivals, haters, the devil. Eleven of the first 17 songs do so, against two of the last 22. The course reads as a slow withdrawal of projection. The enemy outside becomes a “you” to speak to, and in the second term the spell is found inside.

    Concept
    Rites of passage
    A. van Gennep, The Rites of Passage (1909)

    What it says

    Arnold van Gennep showed that rites of passage follow three steps: separation from the old state, a threshold (the liminal phase), and incorporation into the new state. The threshold is often marked by a sign, a word or a ban.

    Where it shows in I win

    The spoken warning at song 12 works as that sign: what follows is not for children. Across it, the vocabulary changes more than anywhere else in the playlist (a similarity of 0.04 between neighbours, the lowest of all). Before it come words of highs and substances. After it they vanish, and “you” rises from 7% to 56% of the pronouns in three songs.

    Whom each lesson speaks to

    Number of songs in each lesson by addressee. The last lesson is the only one with neither an enemy nor a silence.

    The words agree with the reading. “You” grows from 18% of the pronouns in the first eight songs with words to 35% in the last eight. A rise this steady appears in only about 2 random orders out of 100.

    Concept
    Affect labelling
    M. Lieberman et al., Psychological Science (2007)

    What it says

    In brain-imaging studies, putting a feeling into words reduced activity in the amygdala, the brain’s alarm system. Naming a feeling is already a way of regulating it, which is also what DeNora’s listeners do with music.

    Where it shows in I win

    While the soul is alone, the vocabulary of the playlist is thin: 13.7 theme words per 100 words. It more than doubles at the pact (31.8) and stays high through the integration (26.5). The course names more as soon as it starts to speak to someone.

    Concept
    The we-code
    J. Gumperz, Discourse Strategies (1982)

    What it says

    Bilingual speakers often switch to their closer, more intimate language, the “we-code”, to say what matters most. The more distant “they-code” is kept for formality.

    Where it shows in I win

    The threshold is spoken in Arabic, the curator’s closer code. The second term also changes language at its turning points: a confession in Italian, a possession in French. Each switch marks a moment of truth rather than a change of style.

    What to remember
    • The main thing this course teaches is a new addressee: from “them” to “you”.
    • Two signals mark the doors of the course, at songs 6 and 12.
    • The turn shows in the close reading and in the pronoun counts.
    Module 4 · The curriculum

    What does each lesson teach?

    Short answer

    The eight sections form two terms. The first term moves from war to a first glimpse of wholeness. The second term covers the same ground again, one level deeper.

    Analysis

    The curator divided the playlist into eight sections of her own. Read as a school, they form two terms, and each section teaches one lesson (the lesson viewer above).

    The first term (songs 1–17) opens alone and at war. For nine songs everything is aimed at “them”: rivals, haters, the devil. This is the soul at its first lesson, behind its mask, seeing its own darkness outside in an enemy; Jung called these the persona and the projected shadow and placed them at the start of the road. The second lesson is a pact. A promise of loyalty is made and answered not in the language of loyalty but in the language of night and intoxication, as if the soul had to learn that a bond is rarely returned in its own words. Then comes the threshold: a spoken warning, in Arabic, that works like the label on a product, saying that what follows is not for children. After it the words of excess fall away and the songs begin to say “you”. In the third lesson, integration, care given to another comes back as care for oneself, and the playlist glimpses what Jung called the Self, the soul whole for a moment.

    The second term (songs 18–39) begins by taking the words away. Over hard techno, three titles cast spells, and at the exact centre of the playlist stands a title about smelling fear. A confession in Italian and a stretch of silence undo them. The counter-magic follows: a charm is refused, and then the spell is found where it really lives, inside, as a possession, a diagnosis of the ego and its power and, finally, an alter ego that answers without words. Only then does the dialogue return, and with it the enemy, one last time, before tenderness takes over. The sanctuary closes the course. Every song now speaks to “you” or “us”, the sacred words are angels rather than devils, and the last song follows the rivers into the water, which Jung called the commonest image of the unconscious and the place where the soul-guide leads.

    Why two terms?

    A real education does not run in a straight line. The psychologist Jerome Bruner called this the spiral curriculum: the same few ideas are met again and again, each time at a deeper level. Jung said the same of the soul when he wrote that there is no linear evolution, only a circling around the Self. I win draws that spiral. Wholeness is glimpsed early, in the first term, and then circled rather than conquered: the mask returns as magic and ego, the shadow returns as a double and as an enemy, and only after this second pass does the soul-guide lead into the sanctuary. Even the ending stays open, with a song about the stage beside the sanctuary, as if the course could begin again.

    Concept
    The spiral curriculum
    J. Bruner, The Process of Education (1960)

    What it says

    Jerome Bruner argued that a subject is best taught by coming back to it. The same basic ideas return again and again, each time in a richer form, until the learner grasps them fully.

    Where it shows in I win

    The course teaches its lessons twice. The first term (1–17) runs from war to the Self. The second (18–39) returns to the mask as magic and ego, to the shadow as a double and an enemy, and to the soul-guide, before ending in the sanctuary. The second pass is deeper: the spell is now found inside instead of outside.

    Concept
    Circling the Self
    C. G. Jung, Memories, Dreams, Reflections (1963); M. Wärja (1994)

    What it says

    Late in life Jung wrote that there is no linear evolution, only a circling around the Self. Individuation is not a ladder with a top step; it is a path that comes back to the same centre from new angles. The music therapist Margareta Wärja called it the spiralling path of individuation.

    Where it shows in I win

    The Self leads early, in Lesson 3, at 2.3 times its average, and never leads a lesson again. The figures that follow circle around it: the mask, the shadow and the soul-guide each come back once more.

    Concept
    Accommodation and divergence
    H. Giles, N. Coupland and J. Coupland (1991)

    What it says

    Communication accommodation theory shows that speakers who want closeness converge toward each other’s words and style. Speakers who keep their distance diverge, even while they keep talking.

    Where it shows in I win

    In the pact, a promise of loyalty is answered in another vocabulary: night and intoxication. The two songs share little vocabulary (a similarity of 0.13, against 0.34 for a typical answer in this playlist). The bond is met, but not in its own terms.

    Concept
    Two-chair dialogue
    L. Greenberg (1979); H. Hermans (1992)

    What it says

    In emotion-focused therapy, a client speaks in turn from two chairs, for example the critic and the criticised. The inner split resolves when the critical voice softens and the other side is heard. Hubert Hermans’ dialogical self describes the person in the same way, as several inner voices that speak to each other.

    Where it shows in I win

    In the integration, Butterfly’s Repose is the first song where “you” outnumbers “I” (56% against 39%), and its main theme is shelter. The next song, i, peaks on the self and its doubles. These two songs are the closest pair of answers in the playlist (similarity 0.74). Care offered to someone else comes back as care for oneself.

    What to remember
    • First term (songs 1–17): from war, through a pact, to a first glimpse of wholeness.
    • Second term (songs 18–39): magic, a double, the enemy once more, then the sanctuary.
    • The course is a spiral. The Self is reached early and then circled.
    Module 5 · The subjects

    What do the songs talk about?

    Short answer

    Fourteen families of words. They fall into three groups that take turns: the stage, the pact, and the mirror and refuge.

    Each song’s lyrics were scored with a lexicon of 617 weighted words and word stems, sorted into 14 themes. A theme score counts theme words per 100 words of lyrics, weighted from 1 (a loose link) to 3 (a core word). The 32 songs with at least 20 words in English, French or Italian are scored. The other seven tracks are acts.

    Group the themes by

    Darker cells hold more theme words. Select a theme name to read its card; hover a cell for its value.

    The three constellations and their peak songs

    Songs with the theme: of the 32 scored songs, those scoring at least 1. Peak score: weighted theme words per 100 words in the peak song. Share: the peak song’s part of the theme’s total across the playlist.

    Analysis

    The fourteen themes of the lyrics are the school’s subjects. Grouped by when they rise and fall together along the order, rather than by the songs that hold them, they form three constellations (table above): the persona, a subject of stage and voice; the shadow, the subject of the pact; and the Self, a subject of mirror and refuge. Two subjects are daily lessons: looking and talking appear in more than two-thirds of the songs with words. The others are taught in intensive sessions, where one song carries a large share of the theme. LOYALTY. holds two-fifths of all loyalty words, i two-fifths of the self and its doubles, and I Follow Rivers more than a quarter of the water and the night. The peaks tell the story of the course: the theme of devils and the sacred peaks in a song called Sanctuary, with angels rather than devils, and death peaks in a song about not being dead. In the data the pact’s loyalty travels with highs and death, so it falls in the shadow’s constellation; in Jung’s own terms loyalty and night belong to the soul-guide, which is why the lesson viewer in Module 4 names her there. Magic, the subject of the second term, almost never appears in the lyrics. It is taught in titles and in the order instead.

    Use the toggle above the heat map to compare the data groups with Jung’s four figures.

    Concept
    How a constellation is found
    this course

    What it says

    Each theme gets a rolling curve: its average score over an eight-song window, moved one song at a time. Two themes belong together when their curves rise and fall at the same places. Grouping the 13 themes by their curves (a method called average-linkage clustering) and keeping three groups gives the constellations. They stay the same with windows of 8 or 10 songs.

    Where it shows in I win

    The groups describe when themes are taught together, not which songs contain them. Because they were found on this very order, they are partly circular. Module 8 therefore also tests Jung’s own grouping, which was fixed from theory before looking at the order.

    Concept
    The mirror
    D. W. Winnicott, Playing and Reality (1971)

    What it says

    The paediatrician Donald Winnicott wrote that a baby first finds itself in its mother’s face: being seen comes before seeing oneself. Popular psychology calls it “feeling seen”.

    Where it shows in I win

    Sight and shelter are the only pair of themes written into the same songs clearly more often than chance (a correlation of 0.45 across the 32 songs). In I win, looking and being held go together.

    Concept
    Love as a drug
    H. Fisher et al., Journal of Neurophysiology (2010)

    What it says

    Brain scans of people recently rejected in love showed activity in reward and craving systems that are also involved in addiction. The slogan “love is a drug” has a laboratory finding behind it.

    Where it shows in I win

    Along the order, loyalty and highs rise and fall together (a correlation of 0.76), although they share only two songs. They meet in the pact and again near the end. The playlist places the vow beside the high.

    Concept
    The holy beyond words
    R. Otto, The Idea of the Holy (1923)

    What it says

    Rudolf Otto described the holy as numinous: an experience of awe, fascination and dread that words cannot capture.

    Where it shows in I win

    The order keeps speech away from the sacred (a correlation of −0.69 along the order). The talkiest stretch, songs 21 to 25, holds almost no sacred words, and the most sacred song, Sanctuary, sits in a quiet close.

    Read the pairs as leads. Tested one by one, 8 of 156 pair tests pass the usual threshold, about what chance alone would produce. The pairs matter for the picture they draw together, not one by one.

    Module 6 · Jung’s line

    Does the order draw Jung’s path of the soul?

    Short answer

    Yes, twice. The figures take turns in the order Jung described. Then the course circles back and draws the path again.

    In Aion (1951), Jung presents the figures in the order a person tends to meet them: the persona, the shadow, the soul-guide (anima) and the Self. He also warned that the path is not straight. This module follows the four figures along the 32 songs with words.

    Concept
    Individuation
    C. G. Jung, Aion (1951)

    What it says

    Individuation is Jung’s name for becoming a whole person. It works by integrating what was split off rather than by adding something new. In Aion the chapters meet the figures in turn: first the shadow, then the anima and animus, then the Self. This course calls that order Jung’s line.

    Where it shows in I win

    In the first term, the leading figure follows that order lesson by lesson: the mask and the shadow (Lesson 1), the soul-guide (Lesson 2), then the Self (Lesson 3). The second term draws it again.

    Concept
    Reading a level
    this course

    What it says

    A figure’s level is the average of its themes, each divided by its own average across the playlist. A level of 2 means the figure’s words are twice as frequent as usual; 0.5 means half as frequent. The curves average eight neighbouring songs so that the shape of the course shows, rather than the noise of single songs.

    Where it shows in I win

    The three data curves are weakly anti-correlated (from −0.07 to −0.34): when one rises, another tends to fall. The figures take turns in a relay instead of singing together.

    Each point averages the songs in an eight-song window around it. A level of 1 is the figure’s average across the playlist. Numbers along the top mark the lessons; Lesson 6 has no words and does not appear. Hover or tap to read the values.

    Which figure leads each lesson

    Lesson 1. The mask and the shadow open together. The Self is almost absent.

    Lesson 2. The soul-guide surges to nearly five times its average, the strongest lead of the course.

    Lesson 3. The Self takes over, and the first turn ends with a glimpse of wholeness.

    Lessons 4 and 5. The mask comes back, as magic and as ego.

    Lessons 7 and 8. The soul-guide leads the return to dialogue and the way into the sanctuary. The shadow rises once more before the end.

    What to remember
    • The figures form a relay: one rises as another falls.
    • The path is drawn twice, as a spiral.
    • The Self is reached in Lesson 3 and then circled, never held for long.
    Module 7 · The wordless middle

    What happens when the words stop?

    Short answer

    The course turns to magic. Nine tracks move from casting spells to finding the spell inside.

    Songs 18 to 26, grouped by what each track does. Select a track to read it.

    Analysis

    The nine cards above open the second term’s hardest lesson. For anything that counts words, the nine songs between the integration and the return to dialogue are almost blind: three are titles over techno, two have no words at all, and the rest speak Italian, English and French. Their meaning lies in what each song does. Three titles cast a spell, a magic word, a command and the scent of prey, and the third, I SMELL FEAR, stands at the exact centre of the playlist. A confession and a silence undo them. A charm is met with suspicion. Then the operation turns inward: a possession, a diagnosis of the ego’s power and a double without words. The lesson is that the spell was never only outside. Anthropologists describe the same cure in Mediterranean rites of possession, tarantism and the Gnawa lila, where music gives the spirit a name and a place instead of driving it out. It is also the part of the course an algorithm reading lyrics would miss, because it is taught in titles, languages and silences.

    Concept
    Trance and possession
    G. Rouget (1980); E. de Martino (1961); D. Kapchan (2007)

    What it says

    The ethnomusicologist Gilbert Rouget showed that music does not cause trance by itself. It gives trance a form that a community recognises. In the tarantism of southern Italy, studied by Ernesto de Martino, people believed bitten by the tarantula were treated with days of music and dance until the crisis resolved. In the Gnawa lila of Morocco, studied by Deborah Kapchan, an all-night ceremony calls each spirit with its own rhythm and colour. The spirit is welcomed and named, not expelled.

    Where it shows in I win

    The wordless middle follows the same steps. The spell is cast in sound and titles, undone by a confession and a silence, then named from inside: a possession, a diagnosis of the ego, a double. The cure is to give the spell a name and a place.

    What to remember
    • The second term’s lesson is that the spell was never only outside.
    • An algorithm that reads lyrics cannot see this lesson. It is taught in titles, languages and silences.
    Module 8 · The shuffle lab

    Could an algorithm have made this course?

    Short answer

    Not easily. Each of Jung’s five checks is a coin toss on its own, but fewer than 4 random orders in 100 pass all five. The curator’s order passes all five.

    Analysis

    Streaming platforms also put songs in order, but they order them for the next minute, not for the soul. Autoplay picks the song most like the last, so it flows without ever turning. A shuffle turns without reasons. A sort by mood climbs without ever coming back. None of them can teach, because teaching needs a lesson placed at the right moment and a return to what was not yet learned. The playlist is not smoother than a shuffle; it is better placed. Its art is placement: a warning where the meaning changes, a silence where words would fail, a monster kept beside the shelter instead of thrown out, an answer given in another language. Mix makers call such a turn the alarm clock in the mix. In a school it is called a lesson.

      What the curator does that machines do not

      Smoothness is not the goal. The curator’s order is about as smooth as a shuffle (0.07 against 0.00 on a scale where 1 means every song resembles the last). Autoplay is far smoother (about 0.56) and still rarely draws the path.

      Placement is. The two signals sit where the vocabulary turns most sharply. The songs on either side of them share very few theme words (similarity 0.10, against 0.39 in a typical shuffle). Random placements are this sharp about once in 100.

      A mood sort can never bring the shadow back. A sorted line has no second turn.

      Concept
      The shuffle test
      a permutation test

      What it says

      To know whether a pattern comes from the order, keep the songs and break the order. Deal them into random positions thousands of times and count how often the pattern appears. That share is the p-value. A small share means the real order does something chance rarely does.

      Where it shows in I win

      Here the 32 songs with words are shuffled 10,000 times. Each of the five checks passes in about half of the shuffles; all five together pass in 3.8%. The two signals and the rise of “you” are tested in the same way.

      Concept
      Similarity-based playlists
      G. Bonnin and D. Jannach, ACM Computing Surveys (2014)

      What it says

      Most automatic playlist generators choose each next song for its similarity to the last one, or to a seed song, and judge success by smooth transitions. Cunningham and colleagues already noted that such systems sit between loose personal playlists and formal mixes.

      Where it shows in I win

      The autoplay in the lab follows that logic with the lyric themes. It is very smooth (0.56) and passes all five checks from 7 of its 32 starting songs with the data groups, but never with Jung’s own grouping.

      Concept
      Coherence
      H. Schweiger, E. Parada-Cabaleiro and M. Schedl, EPJ Data Science (2025)

      What it says

      Schweiger and colleagues define coherence as one minus the variation between neighbouring tracks, divided by the variation across the whole playlist. A shuffled playlist scores about 0, a smooth one scores above 0, and deliberate jolts score below 0. Across 650,000 user playlists, curators tend to be coherent.

      Where it shows in I win

      I win scores 0.07 on its lyric themes, close to a shuffle. Its art is not smoothness. It places sharp turns where the meaning changes: the alarm clock in the mix that Cunningham’s mix makers describe.

      Analysis

      The lab turns Jung’s line into five questions that any order of the same songs can be asked. Does the mask lead the opening? Does the shadow peak before the Self? Does the Self rise above the mask by the end? Does the shadow fall away and come back? Does “you” grow more frequent? Each question alone is close to a coin toss: about half of 10,000 random orders pass it. Together they are rare. The playlist passes all five with the three constellations, which fewer than one random order in twenty-five manages, and a sort by mood never brings the shadow back at all. With Jung’s four figures fixed from theory it passes four. The mask does not fully lower there, because the close returns to the stage with Movie beside the sanctuary, and four of five is reached by about one random order in five. Both readings agree on the line itself: the shadow peaks at the end of the war cry, the Self in the integration, the shadow comes back in the second term, and “you” nearly doubles from the first quarter to the last.

      Module 9 · What this adds

      What does this work add to what we knew?

      Short answer

      It reads the order of a playlist, not the taste behind it. It joins a close reading, a word count and Jung’s figures in one frame. And it tests every reading against chance and machines, so the labels can fail.

      Six contributions

      1. From taste to order.Spotify’s characters and the psychology of musical preference read what people play and how much. This work reads where each song is placed and what the placement does. The unit is the sequence, which is what curators actually make.
      2. Three readings in one frame.A close reading of whom each song addresses, a lexicon of 14 themes and Jung’s four figures are lined up song by song. Each reading checks the others: the address shows the turn from “them” to “you”, the lexicon measures it, and the figures give it a name.
      3. Labels that can fail.Every archetype claim is tested against 10,000 shuffles of the same songs and two machine orders. Some claims pass: the signals sit at the sharpest turns, “you” rises, and the full line appears in only 3.8% of shuffles. Others fail: single theme pairs are at chance level. A horoscope never fails; this reading can.
      4. A measurable definition of the art.Coherence research asks how smooth a playlist is. Here the curator’s order is no smoother than chance, yet it places thresholds and returns where chance and similarity rarely do. That gives a number to the “art” that mix makers describe.
      5. A bridge between therapy and platforms.Music therapy has used sequenced programmes for inner journeys since the 1970s, and platform studies describe how algorithms sequence us. This work shows a listener writing her own programme with platform songs and reads it with tools from both fields.
      6. A protocol anyone can repeat.The workbook keeps every count live. Another curator can swap in her songs, mark her own sections and run the same tests. The work takes up Sesigür’s call to take curators’ own discourse seriously, and Schweiger’s call to bring personality and context into playlist research.

      Who can use it

      Curators

      A mirror that can be checked. The method shows which parts of a self-portrait come from the songs and which come from the order.

      Therapists

      A listening programme written by the client, with its thresholds and returns already marked, as material for a session.

      Researchers

      Order treated as data, and a reading of archetypes that can be tested and can fail.

      Platform designers

      A shuffle with ordering rules, as Cunningham and colleagues proposed in 2006, could keep a curator’s thresholds in place.

      What to remember
      • Spotify reads habits; this course reads the order and what it does.
      • The labels come with numbers, and the numbers come with a test that can fail.
      • The method is repeatable: any curator can run it on her own playlist.
      Module 10 · Your turn

      How can you use this to know yourself?

      Short answer

      Curate with intention, then test what your order adds. What survives the shuffle is yours.

      1. Curate in turns.Let songs answer one another. Choose for words and mood together.
      2. Mark the thresholds.Place a spoken signal or a silence where you feel a turn.
      3. Name the addressee.For each song, write whom it speaks to: them, you, us or yourself.
      4. Count the themes.Score the lyrics with a lexicon and read each section’s themes.
      5. Shuffle.Keep only the patterns your order makes and random orders of the same songs do not.
      6. Read the figures.See which figure leads each section and whether the shadow returns.
      7. Write back and re-curate.Name what you see, change the order, and listen again.
      Analysis

      This suggests a path to self-discovery that is neither a personality quiz nor an algorithm’s portrait. Curate in turns, letting songs answer one another. Mark the thresholds, with a spoken signal or a silence, where the turn is felt. Ask of every song to whom it speaks: them, you, us or oneself. Then listen to the same songs in another order: whatever disappears when they are shuffled is what the curator added by placing them. Write down what you see, change the order and listen again. Jung’s figures are not a diagnosis here but the names of the lessons, as useful and as provisional as the names of subjects in a school. What they reveal is a movement: from fighting enemies outside to speaking to someone close, and from casting spells to finding them within.

      What this course does not claim

      It reads one playlist, and only 32 of its songs carry enough words to count. A lexicon counts words; it misses irony and metaphor. Whom a song speaks to is a reading, not a measurement. Links between single pairs of themes are no stronger than chance: 8 of 156 tests passed, about what chance predicts. Jung’s figures serve here as names for lessons, not as a diagnosis.

      Keats’s child learns to read with the heart for a hornbook. I win shows a soul learning to read itself with a playlist for a book. Music brings the feeling and curation brings the order, and the order, unlike an algorithm, knows when to come back.
